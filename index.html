Abstract

Agentic LLMs with web search change the threat model for text anonymization: weak contextual cues can become cross-referenceable evidence for re-identification, yet those same details also carry downstream analytic value of the text. Existing defenses either remove explicit identifiers, perturb text for formal privacy, or test rewritten text against non-web inference models, leaving underexplored the operating region between resistance to agentic web-search re-identification and utility retention.

We introduce AURA (Anonymization with Utility-Retention Adaptation), an LLM-powered mask–reconstruct framework that decouples privacy localization from utility-preserving reconstruction and selects candidates with adversarial privacy and utility-retention checks. We evaluate AURA on real-user interview transcripts using re-identification attacks carried out by web-search agents, along with a utility evaluation based on interviewee-profile facts, codebook facts, and the joint contextual utility grid. Our results show that adaptive-scope AURA yields the lowest agentic re-identification counts under each of three attacker models among the non-DP methods, and that at matched scope and backbone, AURA’s mask–reconstruct design retains more contextual utility than the prior LLM anonymizer (+6.4 percentage points in unit-grid recovery) at comparable privacy.

Method

AURA separates the two pieces of an anonymization decision: where to intervene (privacy localization) and how to rewrite (utility-preserving reconstruction). The pipeline runs in three phases, each producing artifacts the next phase can consume — and each amenable to a different model or human-in-the-loop control.

Phase 0 · Initialization

Run a one-off agentic web-search attacker on the transcript to discover not only the base 8 privacy attributes (Age, Sex, Location, Occupation, Education, Relationship, Income, Place of Birth) but also transcript-specific quasi-identifiers — workflow cues, research-pipeline signatures, tool-stack mentions, domain-practice cues, and institutional context. The output is an adaptive privacy scope A, a blacklist B of evidence spans, and a utility insight profile P over 8 dimensions (Theme, Experiential, Affect, Reasoning, Behavioral, Relational, Temporal, Expertise).

Phase 1 · Masking Convergence

An iterative rewriter alternates with LLM-based privacy inference for up to Rmask rounds: each round infers attributes in scope A, rewrites ti to suppress them, and stops when no attribute can be inferred or the round budget is reached. A diff between the original and the converged rewrite yields a masked template T̂ with [MASK_i] placeholders, a mask map M linking placeholders back to original spans, and seed replacements for the next phase.

Phase 2 · Reconstruct & Select

For the masked template only, the reconstructor proposes N candidate replacement dictionaries, producing N candidate rewrites. Each candidate is scored by an attacker (privacy severity S and specificity count C) and a keeper (utility loss L). Selection is privacy-first: filter to candidates with C ≤ Cmax, then minimize (S, L); otherwise minimize (C, S, L) in order.

Variants

We evaluate three privacy-scope variants and multiple LLM backbones:

  • Adaptive privacy AURA — base 8 attributes plus transcript-specific quasi-identifiers discovered by the web-search probe.
  • 8-attribute AURA — the fixed Staab et al. [2025] scope; useful for controlled comparisons.
  • Pure adaptive AURA — directly infers identifiable attributes without the 8-attribute prior.
  • Backbones — Our experiments include GPT-4.1 and open-weight models that can run locally: Qwen3.5-27B and Qwen3.5-35B-A3B.

Benchmarks

We evaluate on 53 re-identifiable interview transcripts from the Anthropic Interviewer dataset. We screen all 1,250 transcripts with an agentic web-search attack and retain cases with verifiable identification evidence. Every defense is evaluated on both privacy and utility.

Privacy · Agentic Re-Identification

Each attacker receives a fresh request containing only the attack prompt and rewritten transcript, without Phase 0 traces. We report successful re-identification counts out of 53 under three attackers:

  • GPT-5.1 (web search)
  • GPT-5.4-mini (web search)
  • Gemini-3-Flash (web search)

Adaptive Qwen privacy scopes are generated with DeepSeek-V4-Flash and web search, holding out all three evaluation attackers from scope generation.

Utility · Three Levels

  • Interviewee-profile facts — 340 validated facts capturing respondent context (occupation, expertise, education, …).
  • Codebook facts — 732 facts from a human-authored codebook with 13 codes across 5 categories.
  • Utility grid (units) — 4,758 (profile × code) joint units; a unit is recovered only when both constituents are recoverable.

Recoverability is judged by DeepSeek-V4-Flash, a different model family from the GPT-4.1 and Qwen rewriting backbones. Grid recovery is weighted over all validated units.

Baselines

We compare against a comprehensive set of baselines spanning the space:

Presidio (NER) One-shot rewrite (minimal) One-shot rewrite (detailed) Anonymizer [Staab+ 2025] DP-MLM (ε ∈ {10, 30, 50, 70, 100, 120, 140})

Results

Across three web-search attackers, AURA’s adaptive-scope variants yield the lowest non-DP re-identification counts under each attacker: 0–7 / 53, compared with 26–40 / 53 for Presidio and 10–12 / 53 for the prior LLM anonymizer. Adaptive-privacy AURA retains 72.0–76.1% of unit-level utility-grid information.

Pareto Frontier

At the same 8-attribute scope and GPT-4.1 backbone, AURA retains 72.7% of utility-grid units, compared with 66.3% for the anonymizer. The paired difference is +6.4 percentage points (nominal 95% CI: 0.1 to 12.7), using a paired transcript-cluster bootstrap with 100,000 resamples. Average re-identification counts across attackers are 10.67 / 53 and 11 / 53, respectively. This comparison holds scope and backbone fixed to examine the contribution of mask–reconstruct.

Adaptive-privacy AURA retains 72.0–76.1% unit-grid recovery with 0–7 / 53 re-identifications. DP-MLM trades utility for lower re-identification, while Presidio and one-shot rewriting preserve more utility with higher re-identification. Table 7 reports the paired utility comparisons.

Why It Matters

The results are consistent with two distinct contributions: scope design improves resistance to agentic re-identification, while mask–reconstruct preserves contextual utility at comparable privacy. The masked spans also provide an inspectable map of the details treated as privacy risks under the chosen scope.

AURA provides empirical protection against the evaluated attackers, with no formal differential-privacy or k-anonymity guarantee. The benchmark is a selected set of 53 vulnerable transcripts; fact recovery approximates qualitative utility and does not directly measure end-to-end analyst performance. Zero observed re-identifications does not establish zero risk against future attackers.

Responsible release remains a process of reviewing residual risks and evaluating multiple attackers. We report aggregate results, withhold identifying evidence and attack traces, and use synthetic examples to prevent localization.
